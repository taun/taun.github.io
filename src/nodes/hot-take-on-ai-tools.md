I’ve been reading the book [Grit](https://www.angeladuckworth.com/grit) by Angela Duckworth. 

“Angela has found that grit—a combination of passion and perseverance for a singularly important goal—is the hallmark of high achievers in every domain.”

I’m not a high achiever, but I have persevered in following a singular goal my whole life. That goal has been, **to make stuff**. But, it hasn’t really been about the making. It was always about the final product, the stuff. If I could have afforded to buy the “stuff” I wanted, I probably would have.

I’ve made quite a bit of custom furniture. Not because I love carpentry (I do) but because I couldn’t find the furniture I wanted at a price I could afford.

My first software driven product was a digital bike speedometer based on a magnet, Hall effect sensor, and microprocessor. At the time, this didn’t exist. I loved cycling, and I wanted a better speedometer. So, I learned enough, physics, electronics, and programming, to make one. The means to an end. 

I attended two software development conferences this fall, [Elixir Conf]() and the amazing [Goatmire]() in Varberg Sweden (more on that later). Both had a strong focus on AI. How to use it, its implications, the problems, etc. But one strong sentiment expressed in the many workshops and individual conversations was, “but what about the code?”, “how do we maintain code quality?”, “how do junior developers learn to write **good** software?”. This is my answer to those questions.

The code doesn’t matter. It is and always has been about the outcome than the journey.

People used to [canoe across the country](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lewis_and_Clark_Expedition) not so much because they wanted to but because they had to.
